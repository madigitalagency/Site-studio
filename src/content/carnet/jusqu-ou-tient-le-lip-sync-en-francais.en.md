## The finding

The Hors-Jeu teaser has seventeen shots. Only three show a character speaking with synced lips. The other lines are played from behind, in profile, in voice-over, or by someone listening. Nobody notices, because a dialogue film almost never shows a mouth in close-up while it speaks. Cinema has always done this; we only applied the rule more strictly.

## Why French resists

The tools hold a short line, in English, on a well-lit face to camera. Move away from that and it slips: French has nasal vowels and liaisons the models know less well, a long sentence accumulates offsets, a three-quarter face loses the reading of the lips. The result is not wrong strictly speaking; it is slightly off, and that is worse, because the viewer feels it without knowing why.

## The six-word rule

A synced line is six words at most, on a face to camera, in a medium close-up, with a voice recorded before the image. Beyond that, we change the shot rather than force it: the character turns away, the other person listens, the camera moves to a hand or an object, the voice carries on. The edit does the rest, and it is exactly what an editor would do with real actors.

## Writing for it

The constraint is settled at the writing stage, not in production. In the shot list, every line carries the way it is played: "sync" if it fits in six words, "from behind", "voice-over", "reading" otherwise. Roger even has a solution of his own: anything long he has to say, he reads, glasses on. We watch his page, not his mouth.

## What we keep from it

We never ask the tool for what it does badly. We write the scene so that what it does well is enough.
